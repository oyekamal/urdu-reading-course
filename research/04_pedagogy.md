# Pedagogy of Teaching Arabic-Script Reading, with a Focus on Urdu

Scope: how to teach *reading* (decoding + fluency) of Urdu — a language written in an extended
Perso-Arabic abjad, normally set in Nastaliq — across ages and starting points: young children
(5–8), older children, adults, Urdu-speaking heritage learners who cannot read the script, and
complete non-speakers learning it as an L2/L3. Urdu-specific research is thin, so most of the
empirical base is Arabic and Hebrew (another Semitic abjad/vowel-marking system), cross-checked
against Pakistani practitioner data (ASER, USAID Pakistan Reading Project) wherever it exists.
Anything not confirmed by a source actually opened is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

---

## 1. Synthetic phonics vs whole-word for Arabic-script literacy

**The empirical record for Pakistan itself is a randomized/quasi-experimental phonics program,
and it worked.** The USAID-funded Pakistan Reading Project (PRP, 2013–2020, implemented by IRC
with Creative Associates, World Learning, IRM) built its entire Grade 1–2 Urdu/Sindhi/Pashto
curriculum around the "five components of reading" (phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency,
vocabulary, comprehension — the U.S. National Reading Panel framework), with Daily Reading Lesson
Plans that explicitly sequence **letter/syllable teaching → letter–sound correspondence practice
→ decodable text → leveled readers**, plus syllable charts and sight-word flashcards as a
supplement, not a substitute. The endline evaluation (NORC, 2020) found PRP:
- increased Urdu oral reading fluency by **12.6 correct words per minute (CWPM)** over comparison
  schools, familiar-word reading by 11.1 CWPM, nonword reading (pure decoding, no memorization
  possible) by 6.4 CWPM;
- increased the proportion of Grade-2 students meeting the KP provincial ORF benchmark (≥60 CWPM)
  by **18 percentage points**;
- standardized effect sizes ranged 0.33 (nonword reading) to 0.65 (orientation to print) —
  "substantive" by education-research convention;
- letter-name recognition and reading comprehension gains were positive but not statistically
  significant, i.e. **phonics-driven decoding moved faster than comprehension** in the two-year
  window.
(Source: PA00Z7MW, "Pakistan Reading Project (PRP) 2020 Early Grade Reading Endline," NORC/USAID,
full PDF read directly — see Sources.)

The nonword-reading gain is the load-bearing datum: nonwords cannot be read by whole-word
memorization, only by decoding, so a real gain there is direct evidence the phonics-based
instruction produced generalizable decoding skill, not memorized sight-word lists.

**Baseline severity that motivated this design:** pre-PRP, RTI's global EGRA review found 66% of
Urdu-tested Pakistani children and 91% of Pashto-tested children **could not read a single word**
of a simple grade-appropriate passage (Dowd et al. 2010a, cited in RTI's "Early Reading: Igniting
Education for All," Gove & Cvelich, full PDF read directly).

**Arabic-specific research (Saiegh-Haddad and colleagues) supports explicit, early phonological/
phonics instruction over whole-word or grammar-first approaches**, but adds two Arabic-specific
qualifications whole-word-vs-phonics debates in English don't need to handle:
- **Diglossia inhibits phonological awareness and pseudoword decoding.** Saiegh-Haddad (2003,
  "Linguistic distance and initial reading acquisition: the case of Arabic diglossia," *Applied
  Psycholinguistics* 24(3)) found that the phonological/lexical distance between spoken Arabic
  dialect and the written/Standard form measurably slows phonemic awareness and decoding
  acquisition in kindergarten/Grade 1 — children are being asked to map sounds of a register they
  don't fully speak onto print. Urdu has a real, if smaller, analogue: many children's home
  register (regional dialects, code-mixed speech, Punjabi/Saraiki/Pashto L1 with Urdu as L2 medium
  of instruction) differs from the "clean" Urdu of primers — this is the direct justification for
  starting with orally-familiar vocabulary in decodables, which PRP's material design already does.
- **Morphological awareness, not phonological awareness alone, is the strongest predictor of
  reading comprehension as texts move from vowelized to unvowelized** (root-and-pattern
  morphology partially compensates for the loss of short-vowel marks). A 2022 Frontiers review
  synthesizing this literature explicitly recommends **embedding morphological instruction from
  Grade 1**, not deferring it to later grades as most Arabic curricula currently do (source read
  directly — see Sources). Urdu shares a large Perso-Arabic root/pattern vocabulary layer
  (masdar/ism patterns, izafat compounds) though it is not as productively root-based as Arabic —
  this argues for teaching common word-family patterns (e.g., -kaar, -daar, mu-, -iyat) once
  learners are past pure letter-sound decoding, not for delaying phonics.

**Verdict:** synthetic-phonics-first, decodable-text-early is the evidence-backed default for
children; whole-word/sight-word lists should be a *small* supplementary layer (very high-frequency
function words: کہ, سے, پر, کا/کی/کے) not the primary method, matching PRP's own material design
(phonics + a supplemental sight-word flashcard set, not sight-words as the spine).

## 2. Letter-teaching order: alphabetical vs shape-family vs frequency-first

Three traditions, three different rationales, no head-to-head Urdu trial found:

- **Alphabetical/qaida order** (Noorani Qaida and most Pakistani Urdu qaidas): follows the
  traditional Arabic abjad-derived sequence (ا ب پ ت ٹ ث ج چ ح خ ...). Rationale is *religious/
  script continuity* (a child who learns the Noorani Qaida sequence can transfer directly into
  Qur'anic reading) and *cultural familiarity* — every Pakistani parent/teacher already knows this
  order, so home reinforcement is frictionless. It is NOT optimized for minimizing visual
  confusion early (ب پ ت ٹ ث ن ی — six single-dot-count-differentiated letters — appear in the
  first third of the sequence) nor for getting to real words fast.
- **Shape-family grouping** (used by *Alif Baa*, the standard US university-level MSA textbook,
  Al-Kitaab series, Georgetown University Press): groups letters by shared base-shape/rasm first
  (ب ت ث ن ي share one base tooth-shape distinguished only by dot count/position; د ذ share a
  shape; ر ز share a shape), teaching the family as a unit so the learner's error-analysis anchor
  is "which family, then which dot pattern" rather than 28 unrelated shapes. Rationale, stated
  explicitly in the textbook's own pedagogy notes, is to reduce simultaneous unrelated visual load
  by front-loading the confusion where it's cheapest to correct (a controlled comparison, not an
  RCT — this is instructional-design rationale from the authors, not a published efficacy study; I
  could not find a published RCT comparing shape-grouped vs alphabetical order for retention —
  **UNVERIFIED empirically**, though it is consistent with the visual-orthographic-complexity
  findings in item 3 below).
- **High-frequency-first / decodable-words-ASAP** (implicit in PRP's Daily Reading Lesson Plan
  design and in most EGRA-informed low-resource-country reading programs, e.g. the "Sound It Out"
  SMRS-style scripted-lesson model referenced in RTI's report): teach the small set of letters
  that combine into real, frequent words fastest, so children are reading connected text within
  days, not weeks. This is a *reading-motivation and time-on-decodable-text* rationale backed by
  the PRP outcome data above (nonword + familiar-word CWPM gains), not by a letter-order RCT per
  se — PRP's own scripted lessons interleave a handful of high-utility letters with immediate
  decodable-word practice from lesson 1, rather than completing the full 40+ Urdu letter set
  before introducing words.

**No study directly pits alphabetical vs shape-family vs frequency-first order against each other
for Urdu or Arabic reading outcomes** — this is a genuine evidence gap, not a settled question.
**Recommendation given the gap:** hybridize rather than pick one purely — use frequency-first
sequencing for which letters unlock real words soonest (motivation + PRP-consistent), but apply
shape-family *clustering* within that sequence when introducing visually confusable letters
(teach ب پ ت ٹ ث ن as an explicit "dot-family" unit with contrastive practice, not scattered across
weeks) rather than the traditional qaida's mostly-alphabetical spacing of them. Keep pure
alphabetical-qaida order as an option/track for families who want direct Qur'anic-Qaida
continuity — this is a real, stated priority for many Pakistani parents and shouldn't be
dismissed as pedagogically inferior without an outcome study to back that claim.

## 3. Positional forms: all four at once, or isolated-then-joining?

The Noorani Qaida and most Pakistani/South Asian qaida traditions teach the **isolated form
first**, drill isolated-letter recognition and sounding to fluency, and only then move to
joined/positional forms and full words (source: multiple Noorani Qaida course descriptions read;
methodologically consistent across quranicma.com, equranschool.com, azharelquran.com — practitioner
consensus, not a controlled study). *Alif Baa* teaches all **four forms together per letter** from
the letter's first introduction (isolated, initial, medial, final shown side by side in one frame)
on the theory that connected script is the actual target skill and isolated forms alone
under-prepare learners for real text — but frames this as a design choice for adult/university
L2 learners already literate in another script, not evidence from a comparison study.

**What the empirical visual-orthography research says about the cognitive cost of positional
forms** (Frontiers in Psychology, 2020, "Learning to Spell in Arabic: The Impact of
Script-Specific Visual Complexity," read directly — PMC7497809): four features make Arabic/Urdu
script visually harder than Latin script for a learner —
1. **letter-shape similarity** (many base shapes nearly identical, differentiated only by dot
   existence/placement/count);
2. **allography** (positional variants — a letter looks different in isolated/initial/medial/
   final position);
3. **ligaturing** (obligatory joining strokes between connected letters);
4. **non-linearity** (diacritics/dots sit off the main line, above or below).

In their child spelling-error corpus, **visual-orthographic errors were 27% of all mistakes — the
second-largest category, ahead of phonological errors (23%)** — and within that category,
**ligaturing and shape-formation errors were the most common, and ligaturing errors actually
*increased* from Grade 1 to Grade 2** rather than resolving with more exposure. The authors'
explicit recommendation: **give visual-orthographic instruction — including positional/joining
forms — real, sustained curricular time from the start of literacy instruction, not incidental
pickup**, because children do not spontaneously master ligaturing just by seeing more text.

**Reconciling this with the isolated-first qaida tradition:** the finding that ligaturing errors
*increase* in Grade 2 is evidence against "teach isolated forms to mastery, joining will follow
naturally" — it argues joining/positional practice needs to be explicit and sustained in parallel
with (not strictly after) isolated-form mastery, closer to the Alif Baa four-forms-together model,
but the study is about Arabic child spelling, not Urdu reading specifically, and doesn't compare
isolated-first vs simultaneous instruction as a manipulated variable — it's evidence about *what
errors persist*, not a direct trial of teaching order. **Recommendation: teach isolated form for
initial sound/shape recognition (fast, low load, matches qaida familiarity), but introduce that
same letter's joining behavior within the same or the immediately following lesson — not weeks
later — and give ligature/joining explicit contrastive drills through at least Grade 2-equivalent
level, because the error data says this doesn't self-correct.**

## 4. Dots and diacritics: when to fade vowel marks

**Diacritics help, not hurt, at every measured proficiency level — the debate is when to remove
them, not whether they help while present.** Alhawary (2020), "Arabic Diacritics and Their Role in
Facilitating Reading Speed, Accuracy, and Text Comprehension," *Modern Language Journal* (abstract
and citing sources read; full text paywalled — could not open full PDF, so effect sizes are
**UNVERIFIED**, but the direction of finding is corroborated across three independent search
results describing it): tested Arabic L2 learners at multiple proficiency stages and found
diacritics/vowelization **positively affected word recognition, reading speed, and comprehension**
— i.e. the intuitive claim that "training wheels" diacritics slow real reading down is not
supported; they support it, including for more advanced learners, not just beginners. A related
Hebrew/Arabic first-language study (Abu-Rabia and colleagues, and a 2017 study "Predicting Reading
in Vowelized and Unvowelized Arabic Script" with Hebrew-speaking children in grades 2/4/6, snippet
read via search results — full text not opened, **UNVERIFIED in detail**) is consistent with
vowelized text being easier/faster for less-skilled and younger readers, with the *unvowelized*
skill emerging later and requiring morphological competence (ties back to item 1's morphological-
awareness finding) rather than emerging automatically from more unvowelized exposure alone.

**Implication for Urdu, which is normally written WITHOUT short-vowel diacritics (zabar/zer/pesh)
in ordinary print** (unlike vowelized Qur'anic Arabic or beginner Hebrew texts): the Urdu qaida
tradition already handles this correctly by front-loading fully-marked text and fading marks
gradually — Noorani-Qaida-style courses teach isolated letters with marks, then aerated
(marked) short words, then progressively less-marked connected text. **The research supports NOT
rushing this fade**: keep short-vowel marks present through early decodable readers even past the
point where a fluent adult reader would find them unnecessary, and fade only as morphological/
whole-word recognition (the compensating mechanism per item 1) is demonstrably online — measured
via decoding accuracy on *unmarked* nonwords, not just marked-word fluency.

## 5. Nastaliq vs Naskh for beginners

**No controlled legibility study comparing Nastaliq and Naskh for beginning readers was found —
this is a genuine gap, and any strong claim here should be treated as UNVERIFIED.** What exists:
- CLE Lahore (Center for Language Engineering, University of Punjab) has published technical work
  on Nastaliq's computational/typesetting complexity (e.g. "Contextual Shape Analysis of
  Nastaliq," CRULP report, opened via search only — this is font-rendering/NLP engineering, not a
  reading-comprehension or legibility study, and doesn't address pedagogy).
- Practitioner/typography-community consensus (Reddit r/Urdu, Urdu font-industry blogs, read via
  search snippets, **not peer-reviewed, treat as informal consensus only**) consistently states
  Naskh is "objectively" more legible for beginners/screen reading because letter shapes are more
  visually distinct and less variable in height/slope than Nastaliq's diagonal, overlapping,
  stacked letterforms — but this is asserted, not measured, in every source found.
- Countervailing real-world fact: **Nastaliq is the culturally dominant, expected script for Urdu
  print** (newspapers, textbooks, religious material) — a course that teaches only Naskh produces
  learners who cannot read the Urdu they'll actually encounter outside the course.

**Recommendation given the evidence gap:** don't treat this as an open pedagogical choice to
resolve by intuition; instead (a) start decoding/phonics instruction in a **clean, geometric
Naskh-style or Naskh-adjacent typeface** (e.g. Noto Naskh Arabic reflowed for Urdu, or a
simplified pedagogical typeface) purely to minimize the extra visual-complexity load documented in
item 3 while the learner is still acquiring letter-shape/dot/positional-form discrimination, then
(b) **transition to Nastaliq explicitly and deliberately once basic decoding is fluent** (roughly
where PRP's ORF "meets performance standard" threshold sits, ≥60 CWPM) rather than either starting
in Nastaliq (front-loads unnecessary visual complexity onto learners who haven't yet mastered
letter identity) or never transitioning (produces learners who can't read real-world Urdu). Flag
this whole section to the person building the course as **evidence-light — a design decision made
in the absence of a direct study, not a research-proven claim** — and consider it a candidate for
the course's own future A/B data if usage scale ever permits it.

## 6. Spacing/direction issues for LTR-native learners; confusable letters

- **RTL/mirror confusion:** no Urdu- or Arabic-specific study on RTL-direction confusion for
  LTR-native adult learners was found in this search pass (**UNVERIFIED** as a distinct research
  claim) — treat this as inherited from general L2-script-acquisition intuition, not a cited
  finding. It is a commonly reported *subjective* difficulty in language-learning forums but not
  something this pass turned up controlled data on.
- **Dot-count confusability is real and documented, not just anecdotal.** The Perso-Arabic
  script's dotting system was historically added specifically to disambiguate letters that would
  otherwise be homographic (arXiv 2210.12273, "Graphemic Normalization of the Perso-Arabic
  Script," read via search snippet: "the inferior dot in ب /b/ distinguishes it from ن /n/ with a
  single dot on top, and then again from ت /t/ with two dots on top"). Combined with item 3's
  spelling-error data (visual-orthographic errors, including letter-similarity confusions, are the
  *second-largest* error category and don't resolve with mere exposure), the specific Urdu set
  ب پ ت ٹ ث ن ی (all sharing the same base tooth-shape, differing only in dot count/position and,
  for ی, final-form tail) is a documented high-risk confusion cluster, not a hypothesis.
  **Recommendation: explicit minimal-pair contrastive drills for this cluster** (item 8), not
  just sequential single-letter teaching.

## 7. Adult heritage learners (speak Urdu, can't read it)

Heritage learners already have the phonology and vocabulary; the task is **pure grapheme-to-
phoneme mapping and script mechanics**, not language acquisition — this should be a categorically
faster course than one built for non-speakers, but I found **no controlled study specifically
measuring heritage-Urdu-speaker reading-acquisition speed** (UNVERIFIED as a quantified claim).
What is available:
- **Hindi-Urdu transliteration bridge courses exist as an established pedagogical category**
  (Columbia MESAAS Hindi-Urdu program; Dr Alam Language Academy bridge courses; Hindi Urdu
  Flagship's "Hindi-Urdu Reader," sources read via search only — course descriptions, not efficacy
  studies) — the field treats Devanagari-literate Hindi speakers as needing a *script transfer*
  course (they already have full Urdu/Hindustani vocabulary and grammar), structurally identical
  to what a Roman-Urdu-literate heritage speaker needs: not a beginner language course, but a
  dedicated script-mapping course.
- **General heritage-language literacy program design principles** (LESLLA — Literacy Education
  and Second Language Learning for Adults — network, source read via search only, not a specific
  study) emphasize: build on existing oral competence explicitly, avoid re-teaching vocabulary/
  grammar the learner already has, and use the learner's existing phonological categories as the
  anchor for new grapheme mappings rather than starting from zero as a non-speaker course would.
- **The single most defensible, evidence-adjacent design move for heritage learners**: since they
  already have morphological/lexical competence (the compensating mechanism for unvowelized text
  per item 1), they can likely tolerate a *faster* fade of diacritics than a non-speaker (item 4),
  because their oral vocabulary lets them disambiguate an unmarked word the way a fluent native
  reader does — this is inference from the morphological-awareness literature, not a direct
  heritage-learner study, and should be flagged as such if used to justify course pacing.
- **Roman-Urdu literacy is not neutral for this group** — many heritage speakers already read Urdu
  fluently in Roman transliteration (WhatsApp, social media); a course can and should leverage this
  by using Roman-Urdu as a temporary decoding scaffold/crutch for word meaning while building
  script literacy, then deliberately weaning it — but I found no study measuring whether this
  helps or creates a competing habit; **flag as an untested course-design hypothesis**, not
  research-backed.

## 8. Spaced repetition, minimal pairs, handwriting/tracing, audio-first vs visual-first

- **Handwriting/tracing measurably improves letter recognition versus typing/viewing alone**,
  specifically for the age range most relevant to this course's youngest learners. Longcamp et al.
  (2005, ScienceDirect, abstract/summary read via search + corroborating Frontiers/PMC sources):
  preschool-aged children (3–5) who practiced new letterforms by **handwriting them recognized
  those letters better than children who practiced by typing them**, with the effect specifically
  stronger in **older preschoolers**. A follow-up fMRI study (James, PMC4274624, read via search
  summary) found handwriting practice produces **early recruitment of brain regions known to
  underlie successful reading** that typing practice does not reliably produce. This is
  Latin-alphabet research, not Urdu-specific, but the mechanism (motor engagement strengthens the
  visual-letter-recognition circuit) is domain-general and directly relevant given Perso-Arabic's
  documented extra visual-orthographic load (item 3): **tracing/handwriting practice for each new
  letterform, including its positional variants, is evidence-supported, not optional polish, for
  child learners specifically** — the evidence for adult learners' letter-recognition benefit from
  handwriting vs. typing was not separately confirmed in this pass (**UNVERIFIED for adults**).
- **Minimal pairs:** no dedicated study on minimal-pair drilling for Arabic-script letter
  confusability was found, but it follows directly and uncontroversially from the item-3/item-6
  error data (dot-count confusions are a persistent, non-self-resolving error category) —
  contrastive minimal-pair practice (ب vs پ vs ت vs ٹ vs ث vs ن, and ی vs ے) is a reasonable,
  evidence-adjacent design choice even without a dedicated efficacy RCT.
- **Audio-first vs visual-first:** not directly tested in any source opened in this pass
  (**UNVERIFIED**). The phonemic-awareness literature (items 1 and 2, National Reading Panel
  five-component framework used by PRP) implies phonemic/oral work should *precede or accompany*
  letter-shape introduction, not follow it — i.e. a child should be able to isolate and manipulate
  the sound before or while learning the shape that represents it, which functionally supports an
  audio-and-visual-simultaneous or audio-slightly-leading approach over a pure visual-first
  approach, but this is inference from the phonemic-awareness-first framework, not a direct A/B
  study of the two orderings for Arabic-script learners specifically.

## 9. Assessment: how to measure "can read Urdu"

**Use EGRA-style subtasks — this is not a course-design opinion, it's the actual instrument
Pakistan uses to measure Urdu reading, with published benchmarks:**
- **Oral Reading Fluency (ORF), Correct Words Per Minute (CWPM), on a grade-level passage** is the
  headline metric. KP provincial benchmarks (set 2015, delegates from all 8 regions, approved by
  provincial authorities; used in PRP's official evaluation — source read directly):

  | Category | Grade 2 CWPM |
  |---|---|
  | Exceeds performance standard | >90 CWPM |
  | Meets performance standard | 60–90 CWPM |
  | Does not meet performance standard | <60 CWPM |
  | Nonreader | 0 CWPM (cannot read a single word correctly) |

- **Full EGRA Urdu subtask battery** (from RTI's protocol, adapted for Urdu by PRP): concepts
  about print, phonemic awareness, letter naming, **letter-sound knowledge**, familiar-word
  reading, **nonword/pseudoword reading** (the pure-decoding check — critical because it can't be
  gamed by memorization), oral passage reading (CWPM + accuracy), and reading comprehension
  (oral-response questions on the just-read passage).
- **Severity baseline for context:** pre-intervention, 66% of Urdu-tested Pakistani children were
  nonreaders by this definition (RTI/Dowd et al. 2010a, source read directly) — so "can read
  Urdu" assessment must include a real floor check (nonreader category), not assume all learners
  are past zero.
- **Nonword reading is the single most diagnostic subtask for "is this decoding, or memorization"**
  — a course claiming to teach reading (not just recognition of a fixed word list) should
  administer a nonword-decoding check, exactly as PRP's own evaluation did, before claiming a
  learner "can read."
- For adults/heritage learners, no Urdu-specific adult literacy benchmark equivalent to the KP
  Grade-2 ORF standard was found (**UNVERIFIED** — this appears to be a real gap: EGRA-style tools
  are built and normed for children). Reasonable adaptation: administer the same subtask *types*
  (nonword decoding accuracy, familiar-word CWPM, passage ORF, comprehension) without assuming the
  Grade-2 numeric benchmark applies, and track relative improvement over the course rather than
  against a child-normed absolute threshold.

## 10. Motivation / age adaptations: gamification evidence

- **Gamification evidence for children is stronger and more consistent than for adults.** A 2023
  systematic review (PMC10591086, read via search summary) and an EdTech Digest summary
  (both read via search only, not full studies — treat conclusions as secondary-source-reported)
  converge on: gamification boosts children's motivation/engagement most reliably **when the
  underlying learning activity is itself meaningful**, i.e. game mechanics amplify good pedagogy
  but don't substitute for it — badges/points bolted onto a weak decoding sequence don't produce
  reading gains.
- **Adult gamification literature is more mixed/qualified.** A systematic review of gamification
  in online adult learning (Kostas et al., JITE, read via search summary only) frames adult
  gamification benefit as real but more contingent on relevance/autonomy-supportive design than
  child gamification — adults respond less to competition/points mechanics and more to visible
  progress/mastery framing and low-stakes practice.
- **Language-learning-specific app evidence** (Cambridge SSLA study on a digital app for child L2
  Japanese learners, read via search summary only) found digital game-based apps effective for
  child language-skill development **specifically when paired with formal instruction**, not as a
  standalone substitute — reinforcing the same "amplifier, not substitute" pattern.
- **No Urdu- or Arabic-script-specific gamification study was found** (UNVERIFIED for
  script-specific claims) — the above is general literacy/language-learning gamification research,
  imported by analogy, not confirmed for this script.
- **Practical implication for age-segmented course design:** children (5–8) — game mechanics layered
  over a genuinely sound phonics sequence (points/streaks/unlocks tied to actual decoding
  milestones like "read your first 3-letter word," not just app-open streaks); older
  kids/heritage-teen learners — competition and visible mastery tracking, lighter cartoon framing;
  adults/heritage adults — progress visualization and low-stakes practice framing rather than
  points/badges, consistent with the adult-gamification literature's "autonomy and relevance over
  competition" finding, though again this last point is inference from general adult-learning
  gamification research, not a direct Urdu-course study.

## What a research-backed Urdu reading course should do

1. **Build the core sequence on synthetic phonics — explicit letter-sound instruction driving to
   decodable text and nonword-decoding fluency — not whole-word/sight-word-first.** Cite: PRP
   endline evaluation, 12.6 CWPM / 18-point benchmark-passing gain from a phonics-based curriculum
   (PA00Z7MW, NORC/USAID 2020).
2. **Use a small supplementary high-frequency sight-word layer for function words only (کا، کی،
   کے، سے، پر، کہ…), not as the spine of instruction.** Cite: PRP's own material mix (core RLM
   phonics + supplemental sight-word flashcards), PA00Z7MW.
3. **Sequence letters by frequency-to-real-words first, but explicitly cluster visually
   confusable dot-family letters (ب پ ت ٹ ث ن ی) together with contrastive practice, rather than
   scattering them across weeks the way pure alphabetical qaida order does.** Cite: visual-
   orthographic error data — dot/similarity confusions are the largest sub-category of a
   second-largest overall error class (PMC7497809); arXiv 2210.12273 on why dotting exists in the
   first place. Offer a pure-alphabetical-qaida track as an option for families wanting direct
   Qur'anic-Qaida continuity — this is a values choice, not a pedagogically inferior one; no
   study disproves it.
4. **Introduce a letter's joining/positional forms in the same or immediately following lesson as
   its isolated form — do not defer joining practice for weeks — and keep ligature/joining drills
   running explicitly through at least an early-Grade-2-equivalent level.** Cite: ligaturing and
   shape-formation errors are the most common visual-orthographic error type and *increase* from
   Grade 1 to Grade 2, i.e. they don't self-resolve with exposure (PMC7497809).
5. **Keep short-vowel diacritics present through all early decodable text; do not fade them early
   for speed.** Fade only once nonword/unmarked-word decoding accuracy is independently
   demonstrated, not on a fixed calendar. Cite: diacritics measurably help reading speed, accuracy,
   and comprehension at every tested Arabic-L2 proficiency stage (Alhawary 2020, *Modern Language
   Journal* 10.1111/modl.12642); morphological awareness is the compensating skill for unvowelized
   text and should be built explicitly before marks are removed (Frontiers 2022 review,
   10.3389/fcomm.2022.984340).
6. **Start letterform instruction in a clean, low-ambiguity Naskh-style typeface; transition
   deliberately to Nastaliq once basic decoding is fluent (~ORF "meets standard," ≥60 CWPM), not
   before and not never.** Flag explicitly to the course owner: **no direct legibility study
   supports this — it is inferred from the general visual-complexity-load findings (PMC7497809)
   plus the practical fact that Nastaliq is the real-world target script**, not a proven
   optimal-order claim.
7. **Run explicit minimal-pair contrastive drills for the dot-confusable cluster (ب پ ت ٹ ث ن ی,
   also ے vs ی) as a standing feature, not a one-time lesson.** Cite: same visual-similarity
   evidence as (3)/(4) (PMC7497809; arXiv 2210.12273).
8. **Require handwriting/tracing practice for every new letterform and its positional variants for
   child learners specifically** (evidence is strongest and specifically age-dependent for
   preschool/early-elementary ages) — typing/tapping-only practice is evidence-inferior for this
   age group. Cite: Longcamp et al. handwriting-vs-typing letter-recognition studies (summarized
   via ScienceDirect/PMC4274624); flag as unconfirmed for adult learners specifically.
9. **Build heritage-learner and non-speaker tracks as genuinely different courses, not the same
   course at different speeds** — heritage learners need a script-transfer course (leverage
   existing oral vocabulary/morphology as the anchor, likely tolerate faster diacritic fade,
   possibly use Roman-Urdu as a temporary scaffold) while non-speakers need full phonics +
   vocabulary + comprehension built together. Cite: LESLLA heritage-literacy program principles and
   Hindi-Urdu bridge-course precedent (read via search, course-description level, not efficacy
   data — flag this recommendation's evidence base as weaker than items 1–8).
10. **Assess with EGRA-style subtasks, not course-completion or subjective self-report** — at
    minimum: letter-sound knowledge, nonword-decoding accuracy (the anti-memorization check),
    familiar-word CWPM, oral passage ORF against the KP benchmark table (>90 exceeds, 60–90 meets,
    <60 does not meet, 0 = nonreader), and oral comprehension questions on the just-read passage.
    Cite: PRP/NORC 2020 evaluation instrument and benchmarks (PA00Z7MW); RTI EGRA protocol
    description (Gove & Cvelich, "Early Reading: Igniting Education for All").
11. **Layer gamification onto genuinely sound decoding milestones, don't let it substitute for
    them; tune mechanics by age — competition/points/streaks for children, progress-visualization
    and mastery framing over competition for adults.** Cite: gamification-amplifies-not-replaces
    finding across child and adult reviews (PMC10591086; Kostas et al. JITE); flag as general
    literacy-gamification research imported by analogy, not Urdu-specific.
12. **Treat items flagged UNVERIFIED in this document (Nastaliq/Naskh legibility, RTL-direction
    confusion for LTR-native learners, heritage-learner acquisition-speed multiplier, audio-first
    vs visual-first ordering, adult handwriting benefit) as open questions for the course's own
    future data, not settled research — don't present course-design choices built on them as
    "research shows," present them as reasoned inferences.**
